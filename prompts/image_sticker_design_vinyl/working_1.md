## The product this style serves

**Intended use:** a single **die-cut vinyl sticker** — one self-contained subject with a bold outer contour, printed at anywhere from small handout size to laptop size. Full colour, flat graphic style, plain background so the cut line is unambiguous.

**Target feel:** playful, collectible, worth peeling off a sheet. It should look like real merch, not a cropped illustration.

**The failure mode to design against:** a poster crop — a subject sitting in a scene, with background elements bleeding to the edges and no clear place to cut.

## What to produce

Six sections. **Budget: about 900 words total, 1,100 hard ceiling** — roughly 100–150 words each, section 6 allowed the most. Tight bullets, numbers wherever a number applies, and cut any sentence that restates this brief back at me.

**1. Composition archetypes**
Exactly three sticker compositions worth using — for example character sticker, emblem or badge sticker, object-with-a-face. One or two lines each: what it does for appeal, how it reads at thumbnail size, what it costs.

**2. Contour and die-cut rules**
The thing that makes it a sticker rather than a picture. Cover: the white keyline border as a fraction of subject height, why the contour should be one simple closed curve rather than tracing every wiggle of the art, minimum concave gap so the cutter can turn, no fragile protrusions or isolated islands floating off the body, and how much the silhouette alone should tell you.

**3. Outline and shape separation**
Interior outline weight versus the outer contour, when a darker tone of a fill beats a black line, how to keep adjacent colour blocks from merging, and the minimum feature size as a fraction of sticker height.

**4. Colour**
Dominant / support / accent split, a working colour count, value separation so it survives both on a white laptop and a dark water bottle, and why muddy mid-tones and over-blended gradients read cheap in vinyl. Flat fills with at most simple two-tone shading.

**5. Charm and merch appeal**
The levers that make a sticker desirable — one clear idea, a face with one readable emotion, a pose with energy, a small joke. Then a short do-not list: generic kawaii filler, sparkles and stars as decoration, dead-centre symmetry, subjects that need a caption to make sense, each with its fix. Note where very short text earns its place and where it just crowds the contour.

**6. Prompt architecture for step 2**
The most important section, so spend your words here. Specify the exact template the step-2 final prompt must follow. Use **short labelled blocks**, not one long paragraph:

```
Format:       die-cut vinyl sticker design, flat graphic vector style
Concept:      one line on the idea and mood
Subject:      what it is, pose, expression, the one clear read
Details:      props, accents, any short quoted text
Line & fill:  outline weights, flat fills, shape separation
Palette:      named colours, dominant / support / accent
Contour:      simple closed die-cut shape with a white keyline border
Layout:       single subject centred, plain flat background, even margin
Constraints:  exclusions, LAST
```

State that the finished prompt should land at **180–250 words**, and that exclusions always come last, after every positive descriptor — a model reading negatives early tends to treat them as things to compose rather than things to omit.

Give the canonical exclusion list to close with: no background scene, no scenery or landscape, no elements touching or bleeding off the image edge, no photorealism, no 3D render, no perspective, no drop shadow, no glow, no gradients beyond simple two-tone, no texture or raster noise, no micro text, no watermark, no signature, no extra text beyond what is quoted, no second subject, no sticker sheet or multiple stickers.

Also state the two things the prompt must say in plain words no matter what: it is a **single die-cut vinyl sticker with a clean white keyline border**, on a **plain flat background**.
